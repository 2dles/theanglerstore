**I was too cautious in the first version of this document. Correcting that.**

You asked whether you can use official brand images or wholesaler images. The answer is **yes**, and there are two routes that cost essentially nothing. This document is the verified path, researched August 2026. Everything quoted below was read off a live page.

> "Is there a fee to be a reseller for CWR Wholesale Distribution? **No, there is no fee.**"

**What they need from you:** "All accounts require a Business License, Personal Guarantee Form, ST-3 or ST-3NR Form, and sales venue information." Activation 24–48 hours.

### ⚠️ The one gap — close it deliberately

I loaded [cwrdistribution.com/terms](https://cwrdistribution.com/terms). It covers pricing, payment, shipping, warranty, and returns. **There is no clause about images, copyright, or how dealers may use supplied content** — neither permitting nor prohibiting.

So the files are easy to get and the paperwork isn't automatic. **Get it in writing anyway.** When your account is activated, email your rep:

>
> Also — how many images per SKU, and at what resolution?
>
> Thanks,
> aj

Save the reply. A one-line "yes, that's what they're there for" email from your rep is the artifact you'd produce if anyone ever asked.

## Route 2 — TopDawg (the only explicit written licence in the sector)

I searched every distributor and platform in your supply chain for language that actually grants image rights. Exactly one has it, in black and white.

From [topdawg.com/terms](https://topdawg.com/terms):

> "**Retailers are expressly permitted to use product images, titles, descriptions, specifications, and related product data provided by Suppliers through the TopDawg Platform solely for the purpose of advertising, listing, marketing, and reselling Supplier Products**"

Their [retailer FAQ](https://topdawg.com/dropshipping/companies-platform/retailers/faq) adds: "**Yes, you are free to customize descriptions, titles, and images to match your brand**," with product data downloadable as CSV.

**Cost:** "To create an account, you only need a valid email address. No credit card required." The START-UP tier is **$0/month**. Paid tiers ($34.99–$139.99/mo) only matter if you want automated integration, which you don't — you're fulfilling manually.

Limits worth knowing: "you will not delete or alter any proprietary rights or attribution notices," and "You agree that you do not acquire any ownership rights in any protected content." Fair, and normal.

**Do this today.** It's free, takes ten minutes, and even if you never order through them, it's the cleanest documented image licence available. ([Doba](https://www.doba.com/site/terms) has near-identical language if you want a second source.)

## "Can we just use Chinese suppliers' images?"

Asked and researched properly in August 2026. The short answer is no, and the reason isn't the one most people expect.

### Copyright isn't jurisdictional in the way this assumes

China and the US have both been Berne Convention signatories since 1992 (China acceded 15 Oct 1992; the US 1 Mar 1989), plus WTO/TRIPS in 2001 and the WIPO Copyright Treaty in 2007 ([US Copyright Office Circular 38a](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ38a.pdf)). A Chinese-origin photograph is protected in the US exactly like an American one.

Two practical consequences from the [Copyright Office Compendium ch. 2000](https://www.copyright.gov/comp3/chap2000/ch2000-foreign-works.pdf):

- Foreign works "do not need to be registered... in order to file an infringement lawsuit in the United States"
- Registration only limits *remedies* — without it they're generally confined to actual damages rather than statutory damages up to $150,000

And a **DMCA takedown requires no registration at all**. That's the low-friction route, and it's the one actually used.

### The platforms themselves say no

**AliExpress**, [Terms of Use §3.2(a)](https://terms.alicdn.com/legal-agreement/terms/suit_bu1_aliexpress/suit_bu1_aliexpress202204182115_66077.html):

> "You will not copy, reproduce, download, re-publish, sell, distribute or resell any Services or any information, text, **images**, graphics, video clips, sound, directories, files, databases or listings, etc. available on or through the Sites"

**Alibaba** §3.2 is materially identical. Both are explicit prohibitions.

**CJ Dropshipping** doesn't prohibit it — it just never grants it. Their [User Agreement §XII.1](https://cjdropshipping.com/user-agreement) asserts the opposite:

> "All services provided on the CJ platform and all included content—including... **images**... are the property of CJdropshipping or its content suppliers"

There is no clause anywhere granting users rights to those images, even though their One-Click Listing feature exists specifically to push them into your store. Their signed [PDF agreement](https://oss.cjdropshipping.com/pdf/CJ%20Dropshipping%20Agreement%202024.12.23.pdf) §III.3 is restriction-shaped and ambiguous — "for purpose of sales only and strictly forbidden to be distributed or used for any other purposes" — and never names you as licensee.

Meanwhile their agreement pushes IP liability onto you (§VII.2: "Party B shall take full responsibility of any dispute"), warns that platform images "may contain risks and defects (including... intellectual property infringement)," and **sells** photography "with copyright" as a paid upsell — which tells you exactly what the free tier is.

### The real risk is upstream, and a licence wouldn't fix it

Nobody found a single documented case of a Chinese supplier suing a dropshipper over the supplier's own photos. Why would they — you're their customer.

The documented pattern is different: **the supplier copied someone else's photograph**, and you are the visible, reachable, US-jurisdiction endpoint of a chain of title that was already broken. That's the [Shopify thread](https://community.shopify.com/t/company-telling-me-i-stole-their-images-i-only-got-images-from-aliexpress/103404) where a merchant got a demand, asked her AliExpress supplier, and the supplier just insisted they owned the images. Unresolvable.

Zendrop concedes this in their own help centre: "**Zendrop cannot guarantee the copyright ownership of all product images**" because many are imported from other platforms, creating "a small risk of copyright infringement."

A licence from CJ wouldn't cure it either — nobody can grant more than they hold.

### What actually works: platforms with written grants

Four platforms grant retailers image rights **in writing**:

| Platform | The exact language | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **TopDawg** | "Retailers are **expressly permitted to use product images**, titles, descriptions... for the purpose of advertising, listing, marketing, and reselling" | **Free tier** |
| **Spocket** | Supplier "grants to the Retailer a non-exclusive licence to download your imagery to upload to their online store" | Paid |
| **Modalyst** | "the retailer has permission to download the supplier's imagery to upload to their online store" | Paid |
| **Doba** | "reproductions, and/or alterations of product images... may be used for marketing and reselling purposes only" | Paid |

**Spocket is the only one that addresses the chain-of-title problem**, not just the permission problem — suppliers must warrant they "hold the copyrights to, or the right to enforce and license the copyrights to" the photos. Modalyst has a similar warranty. That's the difference between a licence that protects you and one that just moves the paperwork.

For this store, **TopDawg is the practical pick**: free, explicit grant, and it has a Sports & Outdoors category. (Spocket, despite the better warranty, has no fishing subcategory at all.)

## Route 5 — your own camera (still the only bulletproof one)

Everything above depends on someone else's goodwill and can be revoked. Photos you take cannot.

You're already ordering samples of `carolina-kit`, `headlamp`, and `landing-net` per `SOURCING.md`. Shoot them.

**Setup:** matte black foam board ($4, craft store) as the background — it also matches the store's dark theme so your photos sit natively beside the illustrations. One big soft light source: a north-facing window on an overcast day. **Room lights off** so you aren't mixing colour temperatures. A sheet of white paper opposite the window as fill. No flash. Shoot from ~30–40° above, phone propped on books, tap to focus on the nearest edge.

**Four frames each**, first one goes in the catalog:

1. **Hero** — whole product filling ~80% of a 4:3 frame
2. **Detail** — whatever the product page argues about (the carbide cutters, the mesh, the red LED lit)
3. **In scale** — with a hand or a familiar object. This prevents the size-surprise returns nobody plans for.
4. **In use** — on sand, on a rod, in the bag. The one that sells.

**Per product:** shoot the headlamp's red mode *actually lit in a dark room* — it's the entire sales argument. Push the sand spikes into real sand at the beach; costs nothing and it's the most persuasive frame available. Fan all 21 swimbaits by colour, because the colour range *is* the product. Rods are hardest (they're 10 feet) — shoot the reel and grip instead, and lean on Pure Fishing's portal for the full-length shot.

**Export:** crop **4:3**, export **1600×1200** JPEG q80, under 400 KB. Name it for the product key.

## Two things to know before you build on supplier feeds

**1. Nearly every automated feed gives you ONE image per SKU.** Inventory Source states it outright: "Currently, we only load one image per product. Our software is not designed to load more than one image." ([source](https://www.inventorysource.com/faqs/)). Kroll and Lipsey's feeds are the same — single primary image. One photo is rarely enough for a product page that converts, so plan to supplement with your own detail shots.

**2. Some feeds carry no images at all.** Land 'N' Sea's EDI file contains SKU, dimensions, weight, title, description, UPC, MSRP, wholesale, MAP, quantity, status — **and no image field**. Sports South's flat FTP feed is the same, though their API path does carry images. D&H charges extra: images are an Etilize add-on, not part of the base feed. Ask before you assume.

**The honest industry picture:** suppliers are generally happy for you to use their photos, and that consensus lives in rep emails and blog posts rather than in the agreements. Where tackle-industry dealer agreements *do* speak, they license **logos and trademarks subject to approval** and stay silent on product photography. That's exactly why you should ask for the written confirmation in Route 1 rather than relying on custom.

## Dropping images into the store

Once you have licensed files:

1. Save as `public/products/<key>.jpg` — e.g. `public/products/surf-rod.jpg`
2. Add one line to that product in `src/lib/products.ts`:

```ts
{
  key: "surf-rod",
  image: "/products/surf-rod.jpg",   // ← add this
  ...
}
```

Commit, push, Vercel redeploys. The photo takes over on the home page, its collection page, the product page, and the cart. Every other product keeps its illustration, so you migrate one at a time as permissions come in.

Images go through `next/image` — resized, converted to WebP/AVIF, lazy-loaded. Export per the settings above and don't optimise further.

**Download and self-host; do not hotlink** even once you're licensed. Permission to *use* an image is not permission to serve it off someone else's bandwidth, and their CDN paths change without notice. `next.config.ts` has no `remotePatterns` configured, so an external URL fails the build — deliberately.
